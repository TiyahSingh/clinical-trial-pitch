Frequently asked questions

What is Clinical Trial?

It is a stylised 3D serious game set in a struggling South African public clinic. You do not build or expand the hospital. You try to keep it functioning with limited staff, supplies, and time.

Why make this as a game?

Our research used a Statistics South Africa figure that about 84% of South Africans rely on public healthcare. Overcrowding, understaffing, medicine shortages, sanitation, infrastructure trouble, and load shedding feed into one another. The game lets you feel that cycle instead of only reading it.

Who do I play as?

You switch between the Manager, Doctor, Nurse, and Janitor from anywhere in the clinic. The camera moves to the character you pick. No single role can keep the hospital going alone.

What does each role actually do?

The Manager buys medication and deals with the budget. The Doctor treats patients, and that treatment improves them. The Nurse assesses patients, sorts medication by colour, and during an outbreak marks who is infected. The Janitor collects rubbish and moves it with a mobile cleaning cart.

When do the timers start?

Each character has a timer, but a minigame countdown starts only when you interact with the prop. Walking up to it is not enough. The round ends when every character timer runs out.

How is the nurse different from the doctor?

A nurse assessment can go either way. If the patient has not improved, you switch to the doctor. The Treat button stays locked until the patient is marked critical or not critical. Medicine has to be bought by the manager before the nurse can unpack it and sort the shelf.

What can go wrong mid-round?

Crises arrive at random. Corruption cuts the budget. Theft cuts supplies. An infection outbreak drops hygiene, comfort, and morale. An outside disaster, such as a bus accident or a building collapse, brings more patients. Stat bars turn orange, then red. Hitting zero funds does not end the game on the spot. You get a chance to recover.

Is it graphic?

No. The low-poly look is deliberate. The project is about systems and pressure, not graphic imagery or stereotypes of patients and staff.

Who is it for?

Teenagers, young adults, students, and players who like simulation or management games, including people who would not usually sit down with a healthcare report.

What happens when the game ends?

An end panel links to non-profit sites about the issues in the game, so a player can read further or support real work after the round.
